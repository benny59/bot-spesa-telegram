Your price index over time

How the index is built

Each receipt is compared with the last time you bought the same products, matched by article number (or by name when there is none). The price change of each matched product is weighted by what you spent on it, averaged over the two purchases, and the geometric mean of those changes moves the index on. A receipt with no repeat products leaves it unchanged. Chaining receipts this way follows the approach statistical offices use for supermarket scanner data. Because it is geometric, a product that goes on offer and then back to its usual price ends where it started.

Each store chain is tracked separately, since the same product can cost differently at ALDI and at HIT. Use the store buttons to see one chain's index on its own. Non-food lines (household, toiletries, alcohol) are left out of every food figure.

Coverage is the share of a receipt's food spending that could be matched. Points drawn larger rest on more of your basket; small points rest on one or two items and can swing a lot on a single promotion.

The dashed lines show where an index starting on your first receipt would be if prices rose at the official annual rate for each month. They are a pace reference, not data from your shop.

Contribution = your spending share × the class's annual rate. Classes marked aggr. have no published sub-rate, so the overall German food rate (or the non-alcoholic beverages rate) stands in. The euro-area figure uses the processed / unprocessed food split, the finest food detail in Eurostat's release.

Where the nutrition figures come from

Receipts don't list nutrition or barcodes. Where the exact product could be identified, its values come from its own label: either a label photo you added, or the label as recorded in a product database (Open Food Facts, fddb or fatsecret). Both are marked “label”. Everything else carries typical values per 100 g for that kind of product from standard food-composition tables, marked “typical”; supermarket own brands are often close to these.

Weight comes from the receipt where the line is weighed, from the pack size printed in the name, or otherwise from a typical pack or piece weight (marked “est.”). For fruit and vegetables only the edible part is counted: a banana without its peel, a mango without stone and skin. Tinned goods count the drained weight. Correct a pack size with Edit under Saved receipts and the figures follow.

Nutri-Score grades are calculated here with the current algorithm (revised 2023/24; milk and plant drinks now count as drinks) from each product's per-100 g values and an estimate of its fruit, vegetable and legume share. A grade printed on the pack and read from a label photo takes precedence. Grades from typical values are a good guide but can differ from the pack by a letter.

The nutrient score follows the Nutrient Rich Foods index (NRF9.3, Drewnowski) per 100 kcal, extended for this page: omega-3 and unsaturated fat count as positives, and grain products get up to 50 extra points for being wholegrain. Fat composition, omega-3 and wholegrain share are typical values for each kind of product.

Vitamins and minerals are typical values from food-composition tables for each kind of product, compared with the EU nutrient reference values. Fortified products (plant drinks, some cereals) and iodised salt can change these a lot; a label photo that shows vitamins is used instead.

Reference values are the EU reference intakes for an average adult (2,000 kcal a day), scaled to 1,000 kcal so they can be compared with any amount of shopping; fibre uses the German Nutrition Society's 30 g a day. They describe the groceries you bought, not what anyone ate.
